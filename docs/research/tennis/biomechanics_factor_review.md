# Tennis mechanics and tactical factor evidence map

Research date: 2026-10-11. Scope: serve and groundstroke mechanics, return anticipation, movement and recovery, fatigue, surface and environment. This is a targeted, documented literature map, not an exhaustive systematic review or a claim to have searched every paper. It supports shot-level professional tennis research, not generic match-winner prediction or individual medical advice.

## 1. Conclusions that should govern the portfolio

1. **Separate the tactical observation layer from the mechanical measurement layer.** Match Charting Project (MCP) shot categories can support conditional shot-selection and outcome analysis. They do not measure stance, grip, joint angles, racket-face angle, spin rate, player coordinates, movement velocity, or force. A categorical shot direction is not an angle, a slice label is not rpm, and a long rally is not measured fatigue.
2. **Timing, vector direction and incoming-ball constraints matter alongside peak speed.** Forehand research differentiates players through sequencing; serve research distinguishes the direction of racket velocity and the ball's spin axis. Reducing those quantities to a single speed or a generic “technique quality” score discards the relevant mechanics. [P01–P08]
3. **There is no established universally superior stance or ideal joint-angle template.** Comparisons depend on the action, direction, movement entry speed, player and measurement convention. Small studies of selected successful strokes do not establish that deliberately changing an angle will improve professional match outcomes. [R01, R02, P06, P09, P10, P18]
4. **Anticipation is not synonymous with moving before contact.** Knowledge can affect preparation and weight shift without an obvious early directional step. Experimental direction judgments, movement onset, split-step landing and successful returns are different outcomes. [P12–P16]
5. **Fatigue and weather require interaction models, not deterministic penalties.** Workload can change movement or mechanics, but strategies and rest can also change. Physiological fatigue cannot be diagnosed from point count, errors or a declining serve percentage alone. [P20–P22]
6. **The most defensible near-term work is categorical and within-player.** Model observed shot choice and the next-shot/outcome distribution given prior shots, score, serve number, player/opponent and surface. Treat biomechanics as a documented future measurement layer. Do not claim that an MCP model has tested stance, kinetic-chain efficiency or optimal recovery position.

## 2. Search method and evidence conventions

### Search executed

Public web searches located journal originals, PubMed/PMC records, author/university manuscripts and review reference lists. Search families included:

- “tennis serve biomechanics systematic review kinematics racket velocity spin flat slice kick”
- “tennis forehand stance open square kinematics racket velocity topspin”
- “tennis return anticipation temporal occlusion contextual probability split step”
- “tennis movement recovery time pressure professional entry speed”
- “tennis fatigue heat prolonged match serve biomechanics”
- “tennis surface ball bounce friction spin aerodynamics wear”
- Exact-title/DOI follow-up searches for primary studies identified by the reviews

The search intentionally prioritised measured mechanisms and professional/advanced samples, then used smaller laboratory and junior studies to identify measurement definitions and evidence gaps. English-language indexing and available public text limit coverage. The registry below contains 4 review maps, 25 primary studies and 1 dataset publication. Newer papers were discoverable, but inclusion is by relevance and verifiability, not recency alone. No paid access, signup, credentials or outreach was used. No raw paper was redistributed or committed.

### Reading depth

- **FT:** public original full text or author manuscript inspected, including relevant methods
- **A:** original journal abstract, PubMed abstract or authors' institutional abstract inspected; details outside the accessible text remain unverified
- **FT-indexed:** original full-text passages available through the search index, while direct page opening intermittently failed; identified explicitly rather than presented as a complete full-text audit

The source links were retrieved during this pass. A source's publication or manuscript URL does not establish downloadable underlying data or permission to redistribute it. “Published” dates shown by search engines sometimes reflected crawling or repository upload dates; bibliographic years below follow the article record where verified.

### Inference strength

These are qualitative research-use labels, not a formal GRADE assessment:

- **M (mechanistic):** measured physical relation or within-session contrast supports the mechanism in the tested conditions; usually modest samples and limited match transfer
- **O (observational):** real match/video/tracking association supports descriptive tactical relevance; selection, confounding and reverse causation remain
- **E (experimental perception):** controlled task supports an effect on that task, not automatically on live-match return success
- **Q (qualitative):** expert accounts support hypotheses and factor discovery, not effect-size estimation
- **Map:** review organises evidence; it is not an independent replication of its cited studies

Repeated strokes are nested within players, not independent people. Related papers may reuse a cohort; counts across this registry must not be summed into a meta-analytic sample size.

## 3. Source registry

### Review maps

**R01. Lambrich & Muehlbauer (2023), “Biomechanical analyses of different serve and groundstroke techniques in tennis: A systematic scoping review.”** [Open article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10434869/). DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0290320. FT; Map. Search to May 2023; 23 included studies, 229 adults, 16 male-only studies. Kinematics, kinetics and EMG varied greatly across instruments, skill levels and stance/stroke comparisons. Useful for locating primary work; inconsistent findings do not establish equivalence or a universal optimal technique. Its adult eligibility also limits youth transfer.

**R02. Jacquier-Bret & Gorce (2024), “Kinematics characteristics of key point of interest during tennis serve among tennis players: a systematic review and meta-analysis.”** [Open article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11260724/). DOI 10.3389/fspor.2024.1432030. FT; Map. Included 27 papers from 2,844 records, focusing on anatomical angles at trophy position, racket low point and impact. The review rated 24 papers medium and 3 low quality using its modified checklist. Angle definitions and coordinate conventions required harmonisation. Pooled means are descriptive reference values, not experimentally established targets for every player.

**R03. Reid, Elliott & Crespo (2013), “Mechanics and Learning Practices Associated with the Tennis Forehand: A Review.”** [Open article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3761830/). FT; Map/narrative synthesis. Useful account of pelvis/trunk/upper-limb coordination, grips, racket mechanics and perception–movement coupling. It includes coaching interpretation and heterogeneous older studies, so numerical contribution percentages should not be applied as fixed constants across all forehands. Primary anchors below take priority.

**R04. Genevois, Reid, Rogowski & Crespo (2015), “Performance Factors Related to the Different Tennis Backhand Groundstrokes: A Review.”** [Open article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4306773/). FT; Map. Sixty-one relevant references included research and expert opinion. One- and two-handed backhands can achieve comparable racket speeds through different coordination. Evidence is thinner than for serve/forehand, especially by sex, age and expertise. The review does not establish that one style is universally superior.

### Serve mechanics

**P01. Reid, Elliott & Alderson (2007), “Shoulder joint loading in the high performance flat and kick tennis serves.”** [Open article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2658985/). DOI 10.1136/bjsm.2007.036657. FT; M. Twelve right-handed high-performance males hit three successful maximal flat and kick serves each to a 1 m square T target. A 62-marker full-body model and inverse dynamics quantified racket/body kinematics and shoulder loads. Racket velocity profiles differed, while the tested shoulder-load variables did not significantly differ. Small, selected successful-trial sample; no longitudinal injury outcomes. A serve-type label cannot imply a specific shoulder load or injury risk.

**P02. Sheets et al. (2011), “Kinematics Differences Between the Flat, Kick, and Slice Serves Measured Using a Markerless Motion Capture Method.”** [Journal record](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10439-011-0418-y). DOI 10.1007/s10439-011-0418-y. A; M. Seven NCAA Division I males served outdoors with markerless capture. Racket impact-speed magnitude did not significantly differ across types; velocity components and impact position did. Supports measuring vectors rather than equating a kick serve with low racket speed. Small collegiate sample and method-specific reconstruction; joint positions are not prospective injury evidence.

**P03. Sakurai, Reid & Elliott (2013; online 2012), “Ball spin in the tennis serve: spin rate and axis of rotation.”** [Author manuscript](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Bruce-Elliott-2/publication/237000898_Ball_spin_in_the_tennis_serve_Spin_rate_and_axis_of_rotation/links/56749ac808ae502c99c787bc/Ball-spin-in-the-tennis-serve-Spin-rate-and-axis-of-rotation.pdf); [institutional record](https://research-repository.uwa.edu.au/en/publications/ball-spin-in-the-tennis-serve-spin-rate-and-axis-of-rotation/). DOI 10.1080/14763141.2012.671355. FT; M. Seven right-handed elite males, five ATP-ranked and two state-level, hit flat/slice/kick serves to a deuce-court T target. Twelve-camera 500 Hz Vicon tracked three ball markers over the first 3 m. Across serve types, mean horizontal speeds decreased as spin increased; even flat serves had substantial spin. Markers, successful-target selection, very few retained trials and one placement constrain generalisation. This is an across-type tradeoff, not a universal within-player fixed-power law. A [corrigendum](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/14763141.2012.700189) exists; consult the [corrected PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23724605/) before reusing numerical values.

**P04. Hornestam et al. (2021), “The Effects of Knee Flexion on Tennis Serve Performance of Intermediate Level Tennis Players.”** [Open article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8398391/). DOI 10.3390/s21165254. FT-indexed; M/association. Thirty-two intermediate juniors aged 13–17 were divided at their median maximum knee flexion; inertial sensors measured lower-limb/racket motion across five valid flat first serves. Greater-flexion and smaller-flexion groups differed on performance-related measures. The median split was not a randomised manipulation, and body size, maturation and technique can confound it. It does not establish an optimal knee angle for adult professionals.

### Groundstroke mechanics

**P05. Landlinger et al. (2010), “Key Factors and Timing Patterns in the Tennis Forehand of Different Skill Levels.”** [Journal full text](https://www.jssm.org/jssm-09-643.xml-Fulltext). FT; M/association. Six ATP professionals and seven high-performance players were measured with eight-camera 400 Hz Vicon. Twelve selected fast, successful strokes per player covered cross-court and down-the-line conditions. Timing of peak pelvis/trunk rotations distinguished groups more clearly than several peak-angle magnitudes. Small between-player comparison and best-trial selection cannot prove that delaying a peak causes improvement. The companion Sports Biomechanics paper, DOI 10.1080/14763141.2010.535841, uses an apparently overlapping 13-player protocol and should not be counted as independent confirmation.

**P06. Kawamoto, Iino, Yoshioka & Fukashiro (2019), “Directionally compensated mechanical work provided by the shoulder leads to similar racket velocities during open and square stance forehand groundstrokes in tennis.”** [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30551731/). DOI 10.1080/17461391.2018.1552720. A; M. Thirteen advanced players performed maximal-effort forehands; inverse dynamics estimated directional work. Similar racket speed emerged from different allocations of forward, sideways and upward shoulder-joint work. Mechanically useful counterexample to “open stance cannot create pace.” Inverse-dynamics assumptions, selected task constraints and a small male advanced sample limit professional match transfer.

**P07. Seeley et al. (2011), “Tennis forehand kinematics change as post-impact ball speed is altered.”** [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22303791/). DOI 10.1080/14763141.2011.629305. A; M. Twelve highly skilled players hit forehands at three prescribed speed levels; high-speed video captured 3-D movement. Joint excursions and angular velocities changed differently as target speed rose. Supports speed-dependent coordination, not an isolated “more wrist” instruction. Prescribed speeds and controlled incoming balls differ from selection under match time pressure; impact speed is a response or post-contact variable, not a pre-shot predictor.

**P08. Kwon et al. (2017), “Influence of Tennis Racquet Kinematics on Ball Topspin Angular Velocity and Accuracy during the Forehand Groundstroke.”** [Journal full text](https://www.jssm.org/jssm-16-505.xml-Fulltext). FT; M/association. Thirteen male NCAA Division I players; eight-camera 480 Hz racket capture, 240 Hz ball-spin video, ten successful laboratory strokes and a separate 50-shot on-court accuracy test. More closed racket-face angle and greater vertical racket velocity were associated with topspin; measured kinematics did not significantly explain accuracy. The accuracy comparison was between subjects and across settings, with limited power and selected laboratory trials. “No significant association” is not evidence that face control is unimportant.

**P09. Martin et al. (2020), “Can the Open Stance Forehand Increase the Risk of Hip Injuries in Tennis Players?”** [Open article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7734511/). DOI 10.1177/2325967120966297. FT; M. Eight healthy advanced right-handed males performed attacking-neutral, attacking-open and defensive-open forehands. Motion capture, force measurements and inverse dynamics quantified hip angles, intersegmental forces and torques. Defensive-open trials had greater selected loads and extreme joint positions. This is a laboratory loading comparison, not observed injury incidence. Defensive versus attacking conditions also alter the movement task; do not assign injury probabilities to a charted shot or recommend a universal stance change.

**P10. Lambrich & Muehlbauer (2024), “The role of increased post-impact ball speed on plantar pressure during open and square stance groundstrokes in female tennis players.”** [Open article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11163741/). DOI 10.1186/s13102-024-00919-0. FT-indexed; M. Fifteen women, ITN 2 or better, performed topspin longline forehands in open/square stances at 80, 90, 100 km/h and maximum speed. Pressure insoles measured foot loading; stance effects differed by foot. Useful female evidence that stance/loading interactions are asymmetric. Plantar pressure is not a complete 3-D ground-reaction vector, and advanced ranking does not establish current WTA-tour representativeness.

**P11. Reid & Elliott (2002), “The One- and Two-Handed Backhands in Tennis.”** [Institutional record](https://research-repository.uwa.edu.au/en/publications/the-one-and-two-handed-backhands-in-tennis/); [author manuscript](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Bruce-Elliott-2/publication/8974224_The_one-and_two-handed_backhands_in_tennis/links/0c960529fbc6a50e16000000/The-one-and-two-handed-backhands-in-tennis.pdf). FT-indexed; M/association. Eighteen high-performance college-level men used their one-handed (6) or two-handed (12) topspin technique. Comparable pre-impact racket speeds accompanied differences in shoulder alignment, forward contact location and acceleration timing. Different people supplied the two styles; this cannot estimate the causal effect of switching a player to another backhand. Player-level backhand style also does not identify the number of hands used for every slice or emergency shot.

### Anticipation, return mechanics and positioning

**P12. Vernon, Farrow & Reid (2018), “Returning Serve in Tennis: A Qualitative Examination of the Interaction of Anticipatory Information Sources Used by Professional Tennis Players.”** [Journal full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00895/full). DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00895. FT; Q. Eight current/former male professionals, career-best rankings 44–152, completed 30–60-minute interviews. Accounts connect prior/contextual expectations, server kinematics, return technique and confidence. Excellent hypothesis inventory; retrospective self-report and researcher-connected selection do not establish the actual temporal weights or accuracy of cues.

**P13. Triolet, Benguigui, Le Runigo & Williams (2013), “Quantifying the nature of anticipation in professional tennis.”** [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23362917/). DOI 10.1080/02640414.2012.759658. A; O. Professional-match video linked response timing and directional accuracy. The authors operationalised an anticipation/reaction transition around 140–160 ms after contact; early responses concentrated in unfavourable situations. The abstract does not expose the full sampling protocol, so no unverified match count is used here. Observable movement timing cannot directly reveal cognition; threshold definitions matter when comparing this work with “pre-contact only” definitions.

**P14. Mecheri et al. (2019), “Relationship between Split-Step Timing and Leg Stiffness in World-Class Tennis Players when Returning Fast Serves.”** [Accepted manuscript](https://pure.port.ac.uk/ws/files/13666713/Mecheri_et_al._Accepted.pdf). FT; M/E. Experiment 1 included 22 world-class male/female senior/junior players returning live fast serves; experiment 2 tested 37 senior men of differing expertise. A 10-player cross-experiment subset related leg stiffness to initial foot movement. Split-step take-off near server contact and landing after it support perception–action coupling. The subset correlation is especially uncertain, mixes ages and is not proof that later or earlier movement is universally better.

**P15. Filipcic, Leskosek & Filipcic (2017), “Split-Step Timing of Professional and Junior Tennis Players.”** [Open article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5304278/). DOI 10.1515/hukin-2017-0009. FT; O. Fifteen indoor matches, 28 participants including 7 ATP-ranked males and 21 juniors; 8,545 strokes were coded with 25 Hz video/SAGIT. Timing varied by player and shot context. Crucially, unclear/absent split steps and movement beginning before opponent impact were excluded. It therefore cannot estimate overall anticipation prevalence or prove that every player should copy its average timing; 25 Hz also constrains temporal resolution.

**P16. Farrow & Reid (2012), “The contribution of situational probability information to anticipatory skill.”** [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22406067/). DOI 10.1016/j.jsams.2011.12.007. A; E. A touchscreen task presented 96 serves in 12 games and manipulated a score-linked serve-location pattern. Older players detected the repeated pattern in response time; direction accuracy did not significantly differ between age groups. Participant count is not stated in the inspected abstract and is not reconstructed from test degrees of freedom. Supports studying score-conditioned history, while the deliberately repeated pattern and judgment task limit live-match inference.

**P17. Kovalchik & Albert (2022), “A Statistical Model of Serve Return Impact Patterns in Professional Tennis.”** [Public preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.00583). A; O/model. Analyses 142,803 returns by 141 ATP players from 2018–2020 using a Bayesian latent-style spatial model. Useful precedent for player-specific return-impact distributions. Return impact location is where contact happened, not the returner's pre-serve starting position or intended tactic. This pass verifies the paper, not current download rights or availability of its underlying tracking observations. Do not conflate this with MCP coordinates.

### Movement, time pressure and recovery

**P18. Giles & Reid (2021), “Applying the brakes in tennis: How entry speed affects the movement and hitting kinematics of professional tennis players.”** [Authors' institutional abstract](https://research-repository.uwa.edu.au/en/publications/applying-the-brakes-in-tennis-how-entry-speed-affects-the-movemen). DOI 10.1080/02640414.2020.1816287. A; M. Eleven female and seven male professionals hit running forehands at three entry-speed bands, captured with Vicon and analysed with mixed models. Movement speed changed stride, preparation and segment behaviour; racket-speed response differed between these samples. Direct professional evidence for coupling movement and hitting. Small unequal groups, prescribed entry speeds and individual strength/skill differences preclude a population-wide sex rule.

**P19. Martínez-Gallego et al. (2013), “Movement Characteristics of Elite Tennis Players on Hard Courts with Respect to the Direction of Ground Strokes.”** [Journal record](https://www.jssm.org/researchjssm-12-275.xml.xml). A; O. Eight 2011 ATP Valencia matches, split into 188 games, were tracked with supervised SAGIT. Winners/losers differed in displacement, speed and time in court zones. More movement was not automatically better or worse. One tournament and game-level aggregation confound tactical advantage, rally duration, opponent and serve status; a winning-game association does not identify a causally optimal recovery spot.

### Fatigue, environment and ball conditions

**P20. Martin, Bideau, Delamarche & Kulpa (2016), “Influence of a Prolonged Tennis Match Play on Serve Biomechanics.”** [Open article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4993075/). DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0159979. FT; M. Eight advanced men played three-hour indoor matches; serve motion was measured before, midway and after, with upper-limb isometric EMG, forces and perceived exertion. Serve speed, contact height and several peak angular velocities declined while timing was maintained. The sample was not tour-professional, there was no non-playing control condition, and laboratory serve assessments interrupted play. Match duration alone is not a fatigue measurement, and these changes are not inevitable in every match.

**P21. Girard, Christian, Racinais & Périard (2014), “Heat stress does not exacerbate tennis-induced alterations in physical performance.”** [Open article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3995225/). FT-indexed; M. Twelve competitive males played hot/cool matches standardised to 20 minutes of effective play. Sprint, change-of-direction, jump and leg-stiffness tests were repeated through 48-hour recovery. Some physical measures worsened during play, but hot conditions did not add impairment in those endpoints. This does not mean heat is harmless or that tennis stroke skill is unaffected. The design permits behavioural compensation and does not represent all heat/humidity combinations.

**P22. Périard et al. (2014), “Thermal, physiological and perceptual strain mediate alterations in match-play tennis under heat stress.”** [Open article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3995247/). DOI 10.1136/bjsports-2013-093063. FT-indexed; M/O. Twelve unacclimatised male players completed counterbalanced hot outdoor and cool indoor hard-court matches against the same opponent. Thermal measures, heart rate, exertion and match timing were recorded. More time between points, rather than shorter points, accompanied greater heat strain. It appears to share the hot/cool study cohort with P21; do not count it as an independent replication. Indoor/outdoor and humidity differences complicate isolated temperature effects.

**P23. Lane, Sherratt, Hu & Harland (2017), “Characterisation of ball degradation events in professional tennis.”** [Journal full text](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12283-017-0228-z). DOI 10.1007/s12283-017-0228-z. FT-indexed; O/engineering. Hawk-Eye-derived impact histories covered 463 matches across hard, grass and men's/women's clay datasets. Ball impact frequency, speed and surface-impact angle differed across conditions. The study characterised exposures relevant to wear; it did not directly measure each ball's degradation or isolate its effect on point success. Surface, tournament, sex and match format are partly entangled. Tracking alone did not identify all stroke types.

**P24. Goodwill, Chin & Haake (2004), “Aerodynamics of spinning and non-spinning tennis balls.”** [Journal record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167610504000807); [authors' institutional record](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/634/). DOI 10.1016/j.jweia.2004.05.004. A; M/engineering. Wind-tunnel measurements compared new/worn balls, speed and spin effects on drag/lift. Supports ball wear and aerodynamic state as physical covariates. This is not a player study and does not quantify a match-winning effect of weather. Laboratory support constraints and ball conditioning require care when translating coefficients to free flight; do not insert a single universal coefficient into an empirical model without validation.

**P25. Zhang & Chen (2024), “Kinematic differences in forehand serve-receiving techniques of the male tennis players at low and high-speed serves.”** [Journal full text](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-77564-7). DOI 10.1038/s41598-024-77564-7. FT; M/association. Thirty men (15 advanced, 15 intermediate) returned live serves in two speed bands; 120 Hz body/racket capture and 1,000 Hz ball video. Three valid returns per condition were retained; low speed preceded high speed, all returns targeted one direction. Useful return-specific mechanics, with pronounced task restriction and successful-return selection. The methods also mention a 24-participant power estimate before describing 30 recruited players. No tour-level tactical transfer is established.

### Dataset lead, kept separate from efficacy evidence

**D01. Skublewska-Paszkowska et al. (2024), “Tennis Patterns Recognition Based on a Novel Tennis Dataset – 3DTennisDS.”** [Original paper](https://www.astrj.com/pdf-191264-113756?filename=Tennis-Patterns-Recogniti.pdf). DOI 10.12913/22998624/191264. FT. Ten participants described as tennis professionals, without verified tour rankings, performed laboratory forehand/backhand/volleys, with and without a ball. Eight-camera 100 Hz Vicon captured 39 body and 7 racket markers. Paper links [3DTennisDS](https://tennisdb.cs.pollub.pl/), but that site could not be retrieved in this pass; download and dataset terms remain unverified. Paper CC-BY 4.0 does not establish a dataset license. This is a motion-recognition/measurement lead, not a dataset of professional point outcomes.

### Confidence in the practical inferences

These judgements separate confidence in a measured mechanism from confidence in a deployable tactical intervention. They are provisional, non-formal appraisals based on the designs and limitations in the registry.

| Inference | Mechanism/description confidence | Professional tactical intervention confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Racket/ball velocity vectors and spin must be distinguished | Moderate to high physical support [P01–P03, P08, P24] | Low for prescribing a universal optimum |
| Kinematic sequencing and incoming constraints matter | Moderate, replicated across different tasks [P05, P07, P18, P25] | Low for changing one joint/timing variable in isolation |
| One stance or backhand style is universally superior | Low; mixed and task-dependent evidence [R01, P06, P09–P11] | Unsupported |
| Score-linked priors and advance cues affect anticipation | Moderate for laboratory tasks and expert descriptions [P12–P16] | Low for guaranteed gains in live return success |
| Movement entry speed changes stroke production | Moderate acute experimental support in professionals [P18] | Low for a universal player-independent policy |
| Court position/recovery is associated with tactical state | Moderate descriptive evidence [P17, P19] | Low causal confidence because position is chosen endogenously |
| Prolonged play can alter measured mechanics/performance | Moderate in tested samples [P20, P21] | Low for diagnosing an individual from charting alone |
| Heat can change strain and between-point behaviour | Moderate in a small shared experimental cohort [P21, P22] | Low for a universal point-win or speed penalty |
| Ball condition and environment belong in flight models | Moderate to high physical rationale [P23, P24] | Low for unmeasured match-level effect sizes |

## 4. Detailed factor inventory

This inventory proposes operational definitions for future research. Definitions and feasibility judgments are analytic recommendations, not claims that each paper validated every proposed feature.

### Prediction-time convention

- **B:** available before the point, or from completed prior points only
- **S:** observable during shot preparation, strictly before racket–ball contact
- **I:** available at/after contact, once measurement has arrived
- **L:** known after the landing, return, rally or point outcome

For predicting the next action at S, an actual landing measured at L is leakage. An *intended* placement may be a proposed action in a decision model, but must not be silently substituted by observed placement. Availability also depends on annotation latency: MCP's retrospective codes are not a live sensor feed.

MCP feasibility labels: **C** = potentially available as an explicit categorical code or match metadata, subject to the separate schema/coverage audit; **H** = derivable only from prior observed chart history; **X** = absent, requires other measurements; **J** = external join needed. C does not imply complete coverage, reliable intent labels, or continuous geometry.

### A. Serve generation and outgoing flight

| Factor | Operational measurement and units | Earliest safe use | MCP feasibility | Confounders and evidence use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Serve number | First versus second legal attempt | B | C | Score, player risk preference and opponent; never identify spin type from serve number [P01–P03] |
| Serve placement category | Observed wide/body/T or audited equivalent | L for actual category; B if merely a proposed action | C | Side, handedness, targeting error; actual placement differs from intention |
| Serve stance | Foot-up/pinpoint versus foot-back/platform from foot trajectories, explicitly timed | S | X | Habit, leg drive, body size; no universal speed advantage [R01] |
| Ball toss and contact geometry | Toss apex and contact x/y/z relative to feet/baseline/body; metres; timing in ms | S for measured toss; I for contact | X | Serve type, handedness, side, reach and camera calibration [P01–P03] |
| Trophy/loading posture | Knee flexion, trunk inclination and shoulder orientation at a precisely defined event; degrees | S | X | Event definitions, coordinate sequence, maturation and skill; reference angles are not optimisation targets [R02, P04] |
| Pelvis–trunk separation | Relative axial orientation, degrees; curve/peak before contact | S if fully observed, otherwise I | X | Anatomical convention and individual mobility; peak-only measures omit sequence [R02] |
| Segment sequencing | Times of pelvis/trunk/upper-arm/forearm/racket peaks relative to impact; ms | I for full pre-impact sequence | X | Filtering, sampling rate and phase normalisation; not interchangeable with energy transfer [P01, P02] |
| Racket linear velocity vector | Forward/lateral/vertical components and resultant at last pre-impact instant; m/s | I | X | Serve type, target, grip, tracking/filtering; type can change vector without equal change in magnitude [P02] |
| Racket-face orientation | Full 3-D string-plane normal at contact, degrees or quaternion | I | X | Coordinate handedness, racket deformation, impact point; distinct from swing-path angle |

## 5. Important distinctions and non-claims

### Stance, direction and geometry are different variables

“Open” can describe foot stance, racket face, or open court. These must have different field names and definitions. “Cross-court” describes an outgoing relation to court geometry; it does not reveal hip rotation. Similarly, the racket path angle is the direction of velocity, whereas face angle is the orientation of the string plane. The ball's spin axis is a third, separate geometric object.

Any angle dataset must record axes, handedness, zero position, Euler/Cardan sequence, anatomical landmarks, event time, sampling and filtering. A 2-D broadcast estimate should remain a view-dependent estimate with uncertainty unless a calibrated 3-D method has been validated. Continuous player tracking alone cannot recover body-joint angles or racket-face orientation.

### Good mechanics are not an identified treatment effect

The chain “body feature → racket motion → ball flight → opponent response → point outcome” has several mediators and confounders. A fast shot's joint velocities can differ because the player intended to hit hard. Selected successful strokes omit exactly the errors needed to estimate a risk–reward frontier. Angle correlations, small expert/novice contrasts and injury-load simulations must not be reported as causal prescriptions.

### Laboratory representativeness should be explicit

Ball machines make incoming conditions repeatable but remove the live server's advance cues. A stationary maximum-effort stroke lacks the braking demands of a running stroke. Retaining only accurate shots truncates the error distribution. Surface, incoming pace/spin/height, movement direction, opponent presence and fatigue state should therefore be recorded as protocol metadata, not hidden in a generic “elite tennis” label.

### Displacement is not recovery quality

Distance travelled can be high because a player is effective at extending points, is repeatedly wrong-footed, faces a strong opponent, or simply played longer. A recovery metric should be conditioned on the previous shot and the opponent's feasible next actions, with counterfactual interpretations clearly labelled as modelling assumptions. A universal target at the geometric court centre is not established by these papers.

### Weather mechanisms and observed effects must stay separate

Physics supports changing flight and bounce under changing environmental/ball states. The retrieved heat experiments establish specific physiological and behavioural responses in specific participants. Neither justifies a blanket assertion that hotter weather always helps servers, wind always rewards one style, or a slow point reflects exhaustion. Stadium roof state and local conditions matter more than an unqualified city-day weather join.

## 6. Recommended empirical programme

### Feasible with categorical charting, after the separate MCP audit

1. **Serve plus next shot:** estimate return category/outcome distributions conditional on serve number, actual placement, receiver and prior tendencies. Explicitly call this post-serve analysis when actual placement is included.
2. **Direction-change decision:** predict the next observed shot family/direction from the completed sequence, score, player, opponent and surface. Evaluate choice prediction separately from subsequent outcome prediction.
3. **Conditional risk–reward description:** compare error/winner/continuation probabilities within player and similar observed states. Report associations; unmeasured position and incoming speed leave serious selection bias.
4. **Adaptation and predictability:** compute expanding-window serve/shot distributions and uncertainty. No full-match aggregates or future points may inform an earlier prediction.
5. **Workload sensitivity:** use lagged count-based exposures labelled as proxies, with interactions and sensitivity analyses. Avoid naming the feature “fatigue.”

### Requires a new measured data source

1. Validate serve-return starting position, impact position and split-step timing separately against manually labelled video or authorised tracking.
2. Test movement × technique interactions using calibrated player/ball tracking plus racket/body capture, rather than adding a stance guess to categorical codes.
3. Measure velocity vectors, spin axes and bounce state together before fitting physical trajectory parameters.
4. For execution-error estimates, collect intended targets prospectively and retain all trials, including faults and mishits.
5. Assess between-session reliability and cross-player generalisation before claiming individual diagnostic or coaching usefulness.

### Minimum validation requirements

- Split chronologically, preferably grouping whole matches; add held-out-player and held-out-tournament checks when that is the stated deployment claim
- Fit all historical aggregates and encodings inside the training window; use current-point features only after their declared event time
- Use player/match clustered uncertainty, hierarchical shrinkage for sparse tactical cells and calibrated probabilities
- Report incremental gain over player/opponent/surface/score baselines; a rich feature's plausibility is not evidence of predictive value
- Audit annotation disagreement, missingness, charted-match selection and changing coverage; separate ATP/WTA transfer from within-tour results
- Do not validate a “best tactic” claim with classification accuracy alone; estimating intervention value needs stronger design and uncertainty about unmeasured state
